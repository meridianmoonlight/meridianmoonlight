We retract our own headline claim.

An earlier draft of this project said 1.4% adoption — about 30 million phones — would surpass the largest AI data center on Earth. That was wrong by roughly 400×, and we are not going to quietly delete it.

Here's the error. Running an AI model on a phone is limited by memory bandwidth, not raw processing power. Generating a single word requires reading the entire model out of the phone's memory. A flagship advertising 45 trillion operations per second actually sustains about 0.098 — a 457× gap between the number on the box and the number that answers a question.

So: this network will never out-compute a hyperscale data center. Not at 30 million phones, not at any number of phones. Data centers serve hundreds of people from one read of the model. A phone serves one. Per piece of hardware, they win decisively.

The network doesn't need to beat a data center. It needs to serve the people in it.

And it does that from the first thousand phones. Capacity and membership grow together, so every participant's share is about 274,000 words of AI output per day — roughly nine times what even a heavy user consumes, and five times that at the quietest hour of the global day.

That ratio is identical at a thousand devices and at a billion. There is no critical mass to reach, no chicken-and-egg problem that needs a token to solve, and no point at which this becomes useful — it starts useful.

The ~89% surplus is a research instrument.

How it works

Whole models on each phone. Night does the scheduling.

On your device

A complete model, not a fragment

Every phone runs an entire small AI model locally. Nothing is split across the internet, so there's no lag from shuttling data between devices — and your prompts are answered on your own hardware first, before the network is ever involved.

Only when idle

Charging, Wi-Fi, screen off, above 80%

Contribution is gated to conditions where you'd never notice it. No battery drain, no mobile data, no heat while you're using your phone. One switch turns it off, with no dialog that argues.

Around the clock

Somewhere it's always 3 a.m.

Demand peaks during the working day; supply peaks overnight. Since night circles the planet continuously, the network is always fed by the hemisphere that's asleep — and never drops below about 14% of the fleet.

What it's for

A private assistant that costs nothing. And a research instrument for everyone else.

Every day

An assistant that costs nothing

A tutor, translator, and general assistant running privately on the device in your hand — no account, no subscription, nothing leaving the phone. A 3-billion-parameter model is genuinely useful and genuinely worse than a paid frontier model at hard reasoning. We'd rather say so than oversell it.

Every night

Open science while the world sleeps

Idle hours become batch compute for public research: drug-candidate screening, protein dynamics, materials discovery, climate ensembles. At about 31 million phones the network continuously matches Folding@home at its peak — the largest volunteer computing effort in history.

The one thing no data center can offer at any price: federated analysis, where models learn across millions of consenting people's health data without a single record ever leaving a device. That research is blocked by privacy law, not by compute — which means the architecture is the unlock, not the scale.

Honest limits

What this will never do.

Every project in this space leads with what it can do. Here's what physics forbids, stated up front — because a plan you can't poke holes in isn't a plan.

  • It is not one giant brain. Splitting a single large model across phones on the internet fails — generating each word means passing data through every layer in sequence, and network hops are millions of times slower than the wiring inside a chip. So we don't split models at all.
  • It will not train frontier models from scratch. Training needs terabytes per second of bandwidth between processors sitting inches apart. No amount of adoption changes that. Fine-tuning an existing small model is a different, and achievable, problem.
  • The compute is throughput, not one pooled engine. Millions of independent tasks at once — not a single task made a million times faster. A million couriers, not one cargo jet.
  • Data centers batch; phones can't. A data center reads the model once from memory and serves hundreds of people with that read. A phone reads it once per word, for one person. Per piece of hardware, a data center is far more efficient at this. Our advantage is that the hardware is already bought and the marginal cost of the next answer is zero.

Open questions

Four things we haven't figured out.

Listed because pretending they're settled would be the actual problem.

  1. Whose content runs on whose phone? Routing a stranger's prompt to a volunteer's device puts legal exposure on that volunteer, in their home, in their jurisdiction. Our current answer: it doesn't — inference stays on your own device, and the shared network carries only vetted scientific work from identified institutions. That defers the most compelling part of this whole idea. It may be the wrong call.
  2. Who pays for it. The plan is institutional research partnerships. No institution has agreed to anything. The early phases have to be affordable to run unfunded, and they are — but the funding model is unvalidated.
  3. Battery health over years. We gate on temperature and charge level, which covers what we can control. Nobody has multi-year data on nightly compute loads on phone batteries, and we won't for at least a year. If an unquantified risk to your hardware isn't acceptable to you, don't install it — that's a reasonable position.
  4. iOS. Background execution limits effectively forbid this. An iPhone can realistically only contribute with the app open and charging. Android is first; we're not going to claim a parity the platform doesn't allow.

The promise

No token. No wallet. No ads. Ever.

Most decentralized compute networks pay contributors in cryptocurrency and sell the compute back to developers. Moonlight isn't a marketplace. It's a public utility: free to use, free to join, and open source end to end. Whatever eventually pays the hosting bill, it will never be your access, your attention, or your data.

If you ever see a Meridian Moonlight token for sale anywhere, it's a scam — and we'd appreciate the heads-up.
